Compound Signal Harness

An agentic pipeline that turns Ritholtz podcast transcripts into a stop-managed swing book. The model reads; deterministic code decides and trades. Sized at $50 in fractional shares, which makes this an integration test with real money rather than a return-seeking strategy — and changes how the stop has to work.

Start here — no jargon

What this is

You listen to a few podcasts where smart people talk about stocks. Some of what they say is a genuine recommendation. Most of it isn't — it's just news chatter about whatever moved that week. You want to act on the real ones and ignore the rest.

So we're building a robot that does five things:

  1. ListensDownloads each new episode the moment it's published, and turns the audio into text.
  2. Takes notesWrites down every company mentioned — who said it, whether they sounded positive or negative, and how long they talked about it.
  3. ShortlistsEvery Sunday, looks back over the past month of notes and picks the best few names.
  4. BuysMonday morning, puts roughly $16 into each of three stocks.
  5. WatchesChecks prices through the day and sells anything that falls 7% below what you paid.

Everything else in this document is detail about how each of those five steps works, and what can go wrong in them. If you only read this box, you know what's being built.

The one thing that surprised me

Normally when you buy a stock you can leave a standing instruction with your broker: "if this drops to $14.50, sell it — don't ask me first." That instruction sits at Robinhood, waiting. It works whether or not your laptop is on, whether or not our robot is running. That was going to be your safety net, and it's a good one.

It doesn't work with $50. Those standing instructions only apply to whole shares. With $50 you're buying slivers of shares — a fifth of one Nvidia share, say — and Robinhood won't accept a standing sell instruction on a sliver. I checked this against their actual system rather than assuming it.

So the robot has to be the safety net itself: look at the price every 15 minutes, and sell when it sees the 7% drop.

Here's why that difference matters. The broker's version is watching all the time, no matter what. Ours is only watching while the program is running. If the robot quietly stops one morning and you don't notice, nothing is protecting your money — and worse, you'll be assuming something is. That's the main thing I want you aware of before we build anything.

So we buy whole shares whenever we can afford one, specifically to get that standing order. If the week's pick trades at $30 and you have $50, we buy one share for $30, leave the standing sell order at Robinhood, and let the $20 roll into next week.

There's a second reason to prefer this that matters as much as the protection: you can check it yourself. Open the Robinhood app and the standing order is right there, with the price it will sell at. A stop that only exists inside our robot is invisible to you — you'd be trusting the robot to tell you the truth about its own safety net. Being able to independently confirm your money is protected is worth giving up a bit of flexibility for.

When the pick is too expensive for a whole share — Nvidia at $180 against your $50 — we fall back to buying a sliver, and the robot's own watching is the only protection. Those positions get flagged on your daily report so you always know which of your holdings Robinhood is watching and which only we are.

This gets easier over time. At $50 a lot of names are out of reach as whole shares. By the time an account slot is worth a few hundred dollars, almost everything these shows discuss is affordable, and the unprotected case mostly stops happening.

How much goes into each pick

All of it, for now. The whole $50 goes into the single best name that week.

That sounds reckless, but at this size it isn't. Spreading $50 across three stocks means $16 each, which is too small to be worth the effort, and your total downside is fifty dollars either way. Concentrating also means the robot only has to be right about one thing — which is a lot easier to judge than three.

You're adding $10–20 a week, so this won't stay a one-stock account for long. As the balance grows the robot opens more slots on its own, without you having to decide again:

And there's a floor: no trade smaller than $25. Below that the robot reports "no capital available" and waits for your next deposit. A $9 position isn't worth the trade, and saying so out loud is better than quietly making a bad one.

What $50 can and can't tell us

Three stocks turning over every few weeks means roughly 30 to 40 trades a year. That's far too few to know whether the idea is any good — you could easily be up 40% or down 40% purely on luck, and neither outcome would mean anything.

So we split the question in two. To find out whether the idea works, we replay the last year of podcast episodes and simulate what would have happened. That's free, and it gives us hundreds of practice trades instead of forty. To find out whether the machine works — does it actually download the episode, place the order, notice the 7% drop — we use the $50. If something's broken, it costs you fifty dollars to find out instead of five thousand.

Short version: the simulation tells us if the picks are good. The $50 tells us if the plumbing works. Don't read a profit or loss this small as a verdict on the strategy.

What I found when I checked Robinhood

The technical detail starts here. I read Robinhood's actual trading interface before designing this rather than assuming how it works, and four things came back that shaped the plan — three of them limits you'd otherwise hit the expensive way.

  1. Broker-native GTC stops exist — use them

    place_equity_order accepts type: stop_market / stop_limit with stop_price and time_in_force: gtc. Your −7% rule can live at Robinhood as a resting order, not as an agent that has to wake up and notice.

    This is the single most important design decision in the plan. An exit that depends on a scheduled agent firing is an exit that fails on the day your laptop is closed.

  2. But stops are regular-hours only

    The schema is explicit: market, stop_market and stop_limit are regular_hours-only and are rejected when tagged to another session. Placed after hours, they queue for the next open.

    So you are unprotected against overnight gaps. A stock that closes at −2% and opens at −14% blows through a stop at the open and fills well below your intended exit. This is why the daily review still earns its place — not to trigger the stop, but to catch what the stop couldn't.

  3. The data you need for ranking is already on the MCP

    get_equity_technical_indicators computes ATR, RSI, SMA/EMA, ADX and Bollinger bands server-side. get_equity_historicals returns split-adjusted OHLCV and the schema itself calls that "the right default for backtesting." get_earnings_calendar covers event risk; get_equity_tradability gates orders.

    No second market-data vendor for v1. That removes a whole class of API-key, quota and reconciliation work.

Finding 04 — fractional shares and stop orders are mutually exclusive

This is the one that reshapes the plan at a $50 sleeve. The schema states fractional shares are available only on type=market, and dollar_amount orders likewise require type=market. A stop is by definition type=stop_market or type=stop_limit.

So you cannot place a resting stop order against a fractional position. Rule 2 — always set a 7% stop — is not enforceable at the broker when every position is a fraction of a share. The GTC stop that was the entire foundation of the exit design is unavailable.

One ambiguity worth settling empirically rather than by reading: the tax_lots exclusion list references "fractional-share limit orders," which sits in mild tension with the market-only rule above. A single review_equity_order call settles it definitively and places nothing. Worth doing before writing any execution code.

On who presses the button

Your account being agentic-enabled is genuinely useful: it means your harness can place orders unattended, which is what makes Phase 3 possible at all. Every read-only tool — quotes, historicals, indicators, earnings, tradability, positions — is fully available for building and validating the system.

I don't place trades myself, so the order calls come from your code rather than from me. That's the architecture the plan already assumed, and it's the right one regardless: an LLM improvising share counts against a live account is a failure mode you don't want, and keeping execution deterministic means every order is reproducible from the ledger.

How it's put together

One organizing principle: the model reads, the code decides. Language work — transcription, who said what, whether a mention was bullish — is genuinely hard and genuinely a model problem. Ranking, sizing, order construction and reconciliation are arithmetic, and arithmetic belongs in code you can unit-test and replay.

The handoff between them is a single table. That's the contract: the model's only job is to produce well-formed rows in mentions, and the trading system's only input is that table plus market data.

Stack

Python 3.12, SQLite for the ledger, the Claude Agent SDK for stages 02–04, plain httpx + the Robinhood MCP for 05–08. No web service, no container, no queue — this is a single-user system whose entire state fits in one file you can open in a viewer and read.

compound-agent/
  harness/
    ingest.py        # feed poll, audio fetch, GUID dedupe
    transcribe.py    # captions → Whisper fallback, diarization
    extract.py       # LLM → structured mentions (show-aware prompts)
    resolve.py       # company name → ticker, ambiguity handling
    score.py         # gates + composite ranker  ← pure functions
    construct.py     # sizing, caps, order intents  ← pure functions
    execute.py       # MCP order placement, stop pairing
    reconcile.py     # daily integrity + gap detection
    attribute.py     # post-hoc: which signals actually paid
    backtest.py      # replay engine over historical episodes
  config/
    risk.yaml        # every tunable number, nothing hardcoded
    shows.yaml       # feeds, speaker rosters, segment hints
  data/
    harness.db       # the ledger
    transcripts/     # raw + diarized, kept forever
  runs/              # one JSON audit record per trigger firing

score.py and construct.py take data in and return decisions out with no I/O. That's what makes the backtest honest — it calls the same functions the live path calls, so you can never accidentally backtest a different strategy than the one you trade.

Animal Spirits skews macro and ETF-heavy, so expect it to contribute index and sector exposure rather than single names — which is fine, since rule 1 admits ETFs. Diarization matters more than transcription accuracy

"Who said it" is a first-class feature. A guest's high-conviction pick, a host's disclosure that he personally owns something, and a passing mention of a stock that's merely in the news are three completely different signals — and they're indistinguishable in an undiarized transcript. Run pyannote for speaker turns, then map speakers to names using the episode description and voice fingerprints you build up over time.

The mention record

This is the schema stage 03 produces. Everything downstream reads only this, so it's worth over-specifying rather than under-specifying — fields you don't use yet cost one token each and are impossible to backfill later without re-running every transcript.

Use show-aware prompts. What Are Your Thoughts? is a bull/bear debate with a known topic list, so the extractor should be told to expect paired takes on the same name. TCAF is conversational with a guest, so the extractor should watch for the guest's book and for Josh's disclosures. One generic prompt across all shows will underperform four tuned ones by a wide margin.

Step 3 — picking the week's names

You identified this as the hard part, and it is. The specific trap: these guys talk about mega-cap momentum every single week. A naive mention-count ranker buys NVDA, AAPL, MSFT and TSLA forever, which is not a strategy — it's a worse-executed index fund with a 7% stop stapled to it.

The key idea — an IDF term for stock mentions

Borrow from search ranking. A name's information content is inversely proportional to how often it gets discussed. If NVDA comes up in 90% of episodes, this week's NVDA mention tells you almost nothing. If a name that's appeared twice in a year gets eight minutes of bullish thesis from a guest, that's a real event.

So weight each mention by log(total_episodes / episodes_mentioning_ticker). This is the term that turns "what did they talk about" into "what did they say that was new" — and it's the difference between a signal and a momentum tracker.

Stage 1 — hard gates

Applied before scoring. A name that fails any of these is dropped regardless of how enthusiastic the endorsement was. These are cheap to compute and they eliminate most of the ways this strategy loses money mechanically rather than directionally.

The volatility gate is the one people skip and then wonder why they lose. With a fixed 7% stop, a name whose average daily range is 4% will breach the stop on ordinary noise within about two sessions — you'd be paying spread and commission to flip a coin. Either the name is calm enough for a 7% stop or it isn't a candidate.

Stage 2 — composite score

# --- podcast signal, summed over a 28-day lookback ---
stance_w   = {strong_bull: 1.0, bull: 0.6, neutral: 0, bear: -0.6, strong_bear: -1.0}
action_w   = {buying: 1.5, owns: 1.25, watching: 1.0, none: 0.8}
speaker_w  = {host: 1.0, guest: 0.9, clip: 0.3}   # learned from attribution later

mention_score = stance_w × action_w × speaker_w
              × log1p(discussion_seconds / 30)     # airtime = conviction proxy
              × idf(ticker)                        # ← the novelty term
              × exp(-days_since / 10)              # recency decay
              × (0.35 if is_news_recap else 1.0)

S = Σ mention_score
  + 0.25 × S if ≥2 distinct speakers bullish   # corroboration
  − 0.40 × S if any speaker bearish            # contradiction

# --- entry quality: don't buy something already extended ---
Q = 0.40 × pctile(3mo relative strength vs SPY)
  + 0.30 × (1 - clamp(|price/SMA20 - 1| / 0.08))   # penalize extension
  + 0.30 × (1 if price > SMA50 else 0)

RANK = 0.55 × norm(S) + 0.30 × norm(Q) + 0.15 × norm(liquidity)

The Q term exists because of your stop. Buying a name that's already run 20% in a week means you're entering near a local high with a 7% stop underneath — the most likely outcome is a stop-out on mean reversion, having captured none of the move. Entry timing isn't optional when the stop is this tight.

Stage 3 — portfolio constraints at $50

Most of the constraint machinery from a normal sleeve is dead weight here. Sector caps assume you have enough positions for concentration to be a risk; at three slots, every book is concentrated and the cap never binds. Drop it for now, keep the code path, re-enable it if the sleeve grows.

What matters at this size is slot capacity, and slots are a function of account value rather than the calendar. A weekly-entry book with a 20-session hold overlaps roughly four cohorts, so entering on a fixed weekly schedule would fragment the account into unusable pieces. Slots scale with equity instead, and a slot must be free before it's filled:

EquitySlotsNotes
< $1501Full concentration. Current state at $50
$150 – $4002Accumulated cash opens the second slot on its own
$400 – $1,0003Re-enable sector caps here
> $1,0004Revisit leveraged gate and conviction weighting

Read equity from the account rather than modelling the deposit schedule — Justin funds this manually at $10–20/week and deposits may not be fully settled, so get_accounts is the source of truth for buying power, not an assumption about what was sent.

Sizing one entry

Decided: prefer whole shares so the stop can live at Robinhood. Share count is irrelevant to what you own, but it is the sole determinant of whether a real stop order is possible — so it gets to shape the order after all. Fall back to a dollar-amount fractional buy only when whole shares can't clear the $25 floor.

budget = min(available_cash, equity / slots)
if budget < 25:
    return NoCapital("no capital available")

n = floor(budget / share_price)

if n >= 1 and n × share_price >= 25:
    buy(quantity=n, type="market")                  # PROTECTED path
    place_stop(type="stop_limit",
               stop_price = fill × 0.93,
               limit_price = fill × 0.915,             # caps a bad fill
               tif = "gtc")
    position.protection = "broker"
    # leftover change stays as cash and rolls forward
else:
    buy(dollar_amount=budget, type="market")         # UNPROTECTED path
    ledger.stop_price = fill × 0.93
    position.protection = "poller"                   # flag loudly on reports

The else branch catches two cases: the share price exceeds your whole budget (a $180 name against $50), or whole shares can't reach the floor ($30 cash against a $16 share price — one share is under $25, two is over budget). Both land you fractional and unprotected, and both must be visible on the daily report rather than buried.

The protection column is the important one. It splits your book into positions Robinhood is watching and positions only we are watching, and those carry genuinely different risk. Any report that doesn't make that distinction is hiding the thing you most need to know.

This constraint dissolves as the account grows

At $50 a slot, the whole-share test excludes plenty of what these shows discuss. At $150 it excludes less. By the time a slot is worth $300–400, nearly everything they talk about is affordable as at least one whole share, and the unprotected path stops being reachable in practice.

So this is a constraint that bites hardest right now and quietly disappears on its own. Worth knowing before you conclude the rule is too restrictive.

Remaining filters

  • Sector caps: off below $400. With one or two slots the cap can never bind. Keep the code path, gate it on the ladder above.
  • Leveraged products: off. Many aren't fractional-eligible, and concentration plus 3× leverage on a $50 account is not a combination worth testing.
  • Drop anything already held and anything inside its 31-day wash-sale cooldown.

Step 5 — the safety net

Decided: hybrid ratchet. Hard −7% from entry; once the position is up 7%, the stop moves to breakeven; from there it trails 7% below the high water mark. That's the policy. The question is how it gets enforced, and at a fractional sleeve the answer is worse than it would be at whole-share size.

The stop is synthetic — and that is a real downgrade, not a workaround

Because no resting stop can sit against a fractional position, the harness has to enforce the rule itself: poll the price, compare against the stop level held in the ledger, and fire a market sell when it breaches.

This inverts the safety property the original design was built on. A broker-side GTC stop works when your laptop is shut, your network is down, and the harness has crashed. A synthetic stop works only when the polling job runs. Every failure of the scheduler is now a failure of your risk control.

Be honest about what you actually have: not a 7% stop, but "a 7% stop as observed at the next poll." With 15-minute polling, a fast move can take you out several points below your intended exit — and overnight gaps are entirely unprotected, same as before, but now with no resting order to catch the open either.

Mechanics

Entry is a dollar_amount market order in regular hours — the only order shape that buys fractional. There is no paired stop to place, so instead the fill writes stop_price and high_water_mark into positions, and the polling job owns the exit from that moment on.

# every 15 min, 09:35–15:55 ET
for p in open_positions:
    px = quote(p.ticker).last
    p.high_water_mark = max(p.high_water_mark, close_of_prior_session)

    stop = p.entry × 0.93                              # hard floor
    if p.high_water_mark >= p.entry × 1.07:
        stop = max(p.entry, p.high_water_mark × 0.93)  # breakeven, then trail

    if px <= stop:
        sell_market(p.ticker, dollar_amount=None, quantity=p.qty)
        record_exit(p, reason="stop")

Track high_water_mark off daily closes, not intraday highs. A single wick would otherwise ratchet the stop up to a level the stock never really traded at, and take you out on the next ordinary session. Closes are the conservative choice and they keep the backtest reproducible.

One upside of going synthetic: the ratchet no longer needs cancel-and-replace, so the unprotected-window problem and the order-churn threshold both disappear. The stop level is just a number in your database that the poller reads. Simpler code — carrying strictly more risk.

Time stop

Decided: 20 sessions. If neither the stop nor a meaningful move has resolved a position in about four weeks, exit at market on the next open. No fixed profit target — the trailing leg of the ratchet handles the upside, so winners aren't capped. The daily job evaluates time_stop_date and queues a market exit; because that's a plain sell rather than a stop, it needs no special handling beyond the approval gate.

Leveraged products need different sizing

The 3× math

A 7% stop on a 3× ETF is a 2.33% move in the underlying index. That is well inside a single ordinary session. Applying your rule literally to leveraged products means near-certain stop-outs that have nothing to do with whether the thesis was right.

Keep the 7% stop — it's your rule and it's a good one — but size leveraged positions at one-third of a normal position. Same dollar risk per trade, and the stop distance now corresponds to a sensible move in the thing you're actually betting on. Also add decay awareness: leveraged ETFs are not multi-week instruments, so pair them with a shorter time stop.

Deferred at $50. A one-third slot here is about $5, and many leveraged ETFs aren't fractional-eligible anyway. Build the gate, leave it off, revisit if the sleeve grows.

Gap risk is real and unhedgeable here

Stops are regular-hours-only. If a name gaps below your stop overnight, the resting stop triggers at the open and fills wherever the market is — possibly 15% down. Nothing in this design prevents that; the daily review exists to detect it, book the actual loss, and make sure you don't carry a position you believe is protected but isn't.

Frictions that bind at this size

  • Same-day round trips are the thing to avoid, and which rule bites depends on your account type. On a margin/Instant account, four day trades in five business days flags you as a pattern day trader and under $25k that restricts the account for 90 days. On a cash account, PDT doesn't apply but settlement does — reusing unsettled proceeds is a good-faith violation, and three of those restricts you to settled cash for 90 days. Either way the harness needs the same rule: never exit a position the same session it was opened. A tight stop will try to make you do this; refuse, and carry the position overnight instead.
  • Wash sales. Frequent stop-outs and re-entries within 30 days disallow losses across positions. At $50 the tax dollars are immaterial, but the ledger should track it anyway — the habit is free now and expensive to retrofit later. A 31-day cooldown before re-entering a name that stopped you out is good discipline regardless.
  • Spread, not commission. Robinhood charges no commission and the regulatory sell fees round to zero on a $16 position. The only real friction is the bid-ask spread, which on the large, liquid names these shows discuss runs a few basis points. At this size friction is genuinely not your problem — which is worth knowing, because it means a bad result is signal, not costs.

What runs, and when

Scheduling reliability — read this before wiring anything

The built-in scheduled-task tool only fires while the Claude app is open; missed runs execute on next launch. The session-scoped cron is worse — it's in-memory and expires after seven days. Neither is acceptable for a daily stop-integrity check on a live account.

Put anything execution-critical on macOS launchd, running a plain Python entrypoint. Use the in-app scheduler only for the parts where a late run is merely annoying — the digest, the weekly writeup, the monthly attribution report.

Why the ranking is weekly but the buying is daily

These are two different activities and they deserve two different rhythms. Running them on the same schedule is what made the earlier version awkward.

Ranking is weekly because the information is weekly. New material arrives when episodes drop — Wednesday through Friday. Re-scoring on a Tuesday morning produces the same answer as Monday, because nothing was said in between. A daily ranker would just be recomputing the same numbers and inviting you to fiddle with a conclusion that hasn't changed.

Buying is daily because opportunity isn't weekly. If a stop fires on Tuesday, a slot opens on Tuesday. Under a purely weekly cycle that cash would sit idle until the following Monday — nearly a week of doing nothing for no reason. The daily deploy check just asks a narrow question: is there a free slot and at least $25? If so, take the best name still standing on the current shortlist.

The one thing the daily check must do is re-verify before it acts. A shortlist built on Sunday and used on Thursday is four days stale, and the entry-quality filters — the ones that stop you buying something that has already run 20% — need to be re-run against Thursday's price, not Sunday's. If the name no longer passes, move to the next one on the list rather than buying it anyway.

How you check the safety net is actually there

This is the routine, and it's deliberately something you can do yourself without trusting the robot.

Positions bought as whole shares have their stop at Robinhood. It's a real order sitting in your account, and you can see it in the app under the position — including the price it will sell at. The 09:50 job asks Robinhood what stops exist, compares that list to what the ledger expects, and raises an alarm on any mismatch: a missing stop, a stop at the wrong price, a stop on a position you've already exited.

That's a much better arrangement than the robot marking its own homework. There is an authoritative external record, our system's belief about it, and a daily reconciliation between the two. When they disagree, Robinhood is right and the ledger is wrong.

Positions bought as fractions have no such record, and the price poll is the only thing standing behind them. Those get flagged on every report. Give the poll a heartbeat — write a timestamp on each successful run and have the evening review shout if the day's count came up short. A poller that quietly stopped is worse than no protection at all, because you'd still believe you were covered.

Fifteen minutes for the poll is a judgment call, not a derived number. On a $50 position the difference between a 7% and a 9% realised exit is about a dollar, so it isn't worth optimising. Whether it runs at all matters enormously more than how often.

The 09:45 entry window is deliberate too: it avoids the opening auction, by which point the overnight reaction to a Friday episode has largely resolved and spreads have normalized. Every run writes a JSON record to runs/ and is idempotent — re-running Monday's entry trigger twice must not double your position, which is what ref_id is for. Generate the UUID from a hash of (date, symbol, side, intent_id) so a retry naturally reuses it.

How it reaches you

Decided: push notifications. Which is right, but it has a hole in it that needs closing first.

A dead robot can't tell you it's dead

If every alert comes from the harness, then the harness failing is the one event that generates no alert. Silence would be indistinguishable from a quiet, healthy day — and it would arrive exactly when your unprotected positions have nothing watching them.

The fix is a dead-man's switch: the price poll pings an outside service at the end of every successful run, and that service alerts you when a ping fails to arrive. It inverts the logic — silence becomes the alarm rather than the comfort. healthchecks.io does exactly this on a free tier, and it's about four lines of code to wire up.

This is the single highest-value piece of monitoring in the whole system. Build it before you build the pretty status report.

Three tiers, because everything buzzing is the same as nothing buzzing

If routine activity pings your phone, you'll start dismissing notifications without reading them — and then the ones that matter stop working too. Triage them up front:

TierEventsBehaviour
wake meBuy filled but stop failed to place · stop missing at Robinhood when one is expected · price poll stopped running · order rejectedPush immediately, ignore quiet hours, repeat until acknowledged
tell meBought something · stopped out · time-stop exit · gapped through a stopPush once, respect quiet hours
just logNo capital available · weekly shortlist ready · nothing to do todayWritten to the digest, never pushed

Note what's in the top tier: it's almost entirely the safety net failing, not the market moving against you. Losing 7% on a position is the system working as designed and belongs in tier two. A position sitting there with no stop on it is the system broken, and that's worth interrupting dinner for.

Plumbing

The critical jobs run under launchd as plain Python, independent of any Claude session, so they need a notification channel that doesn't depend on an app being open. Two straightforward options:

  • ntfy.sh — free, no account, one HTTP POST, phone app on both platforms. Caveat: on the public instance your topic name is the only thing keeping it private, so pick something unguessable or self-host.
  • Pushover — about $5 once, proper accounts and delivery receipts, supports the repeat-until-acknowledged behaviour tier one wants.

Either is fine. Pushover earns its fiver if you want tier-one alerts that keep nagging until you actually look at them.

Anything running as a Claude scheduled task — the weekly writeup, monthly attribution — can use the built-in notification path instead, which reaches your phone when Remote Control is connected. Keep the two channels separate rather than routing critical alerts through the app; the whole point of the launchd jobs is that they don't depend on it.

The order we build it in

Phase 0 is not optional and it's not a formality. It's the phase that tells you whether the premise holds, and it costs nothing but time.

What $50 is for, and what it isn't

Three slots turning over every few weeks produces maybe 30–40 trades a year. That is nowhere near enough to separate skill from luck — you could be up 40% or down 40% on noise alone, and neither result would tell you anything about the ranker.

So the division of labour is: the backtest is the evidence, and the $50 is the integration test. Fifty-two weeks of episodes gives you hundreds of simulated trades to judge the strategy on. The live sleeve exists to prove the plumbing works — that the poller fires, that orders fill, that the ledger reconciles — with the downside capped at the price of a decent dinner. Those are two different questions and the $50 only answers the second one. Don't read a P&L this small as a verdict on the picks.

  1. PHASE 0~1 week

    Backtest the premise

    Before writing a single line of execution code, answer: does a podcast-mention signal actually have edge, net of a 7% stop?

    • Ingest and transcribe the last 52 weeks of episodes — the pipeline you build here is the production pipeline
    • Extract mentions; hand-label ~150 of them to measure extractor precision and recall
    • Replay: enter next open after episode, 7% stop against intraday lows from get_equity_historicals
    • Compare to SPY, and to a naive "buy every bullish mention" baseline
    Gate: ranker beats the naive baseline AND clears SPY after costs. If not, tune or stop — don't fund it.
  2. PHASE 1~1 week

    Live pipeline, paper only

    Stages 01–06 running on triggers, producing a weekly slate you can read. No broker connection at all.

    • launchd jobs for episode watch and Sunday slate
    • Weekly writeup: names, scores, the quote that drove each pick, gates passed and failed
    • Paper ledger tracking hypothetical fills and stops
    Gate: four consecutive weeks where you'd have been comfortable taking every trade it proposed.
  3. PHASE 2~1 week

    Broker integration, approval-gated

    Real orders with $50 behind them. Every intent requires your explicit yes before it's placed. This is an integration test that happens to use real money, and the money is small enough that the bugs are affordable.

    • Settle the fractional-stop ambiguity with a review_equity_order probe before writing execution code
    • review_equity_order before every placement; surface cost and alerts
    • Stop poller running on launchd with a heartbeat and a missed-run alarm
    • Same-session-exit block, so a stop breach on entry day queues for the next open instead of tripping a round-trip violation
    Gate: one full month where the poller fired every expected cycle and every stop breach produced a correctly recorded exit. You are testing the machine, not the strategy.
  4. PHASE 3ongoing

    Loosen the leash, selectively

    Auto-place entries under a per-trade dollar ceiling; keep approval for anything larger, anything leveraged, and any position sized above the normal cap.

    • Circuit breakers: daily loss limit, consecutive-stop-out limit, global kill switch
    • Monthly attribution re-fits speaker and stance weights from realized exits

Still open

  1. How do you want to handle rule 2 not being enforceable?

    Your stop rule was written assuming a stop order exists. At fractional size it doesn't, and the synthetic replacement is materially weaker. That's a decision about risk appetite, not a technical detail, so it's yours.

    Three coherent responses. Accept the degradation — run synthetic stops, poll every 15 minutes, and treat "7% at next poll" as the real rule. Downside is bounded at $50 and the point of this phase is learning, so this is defensible. Restrict the universe to whole-share-affordable names — real broker stops return, but at ~$16 a slot that means only stocks under $16, which excludes essentially everything these shows discuss and drags in exactly the low-priced, high-ATR names your volatility gate is designed to reject. Stay on paper until the sleeve supports whole shares, and spend the time on the backtest instead.

    Recommend Accept the degradation and go live small. The universe restriction breaks the strategy to preserve a mechanism, which is backwards, and $50 is a genuinely good price for finding out whether your polling job is reliable — that's a lesson better learned at this size than at a size where it costs something.
